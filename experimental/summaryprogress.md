1. Progress/Productivity/Impact
The present project builds on our established pillars: (1) algorithmic foundations for extending optical-flow analysis to complex, non-2D surfaces; (2) large-scale datasets already available; and (3) open-source software that maximizes user adoption and the impact of project outcomes.
Algorithmic foundations. NPA Baillet’s group has extended the classical optical-flow framework2, originally designed for 2D image sequences, to the folded cortical surface using Riemannian geometry3–6. We demonstrated that the governing equations of optical flow can be reformulated in this curved-space framework, making the displacement-estimation problem mathematically well posed for brain data. Importantly, we connected optical flow to the advection equation, which describes how a signal evolves under a known flow field3. This duality (retrieving motion from neural activity patterns, and predicting signal evolution given a velocity field) opened a powerful conceptual bridge between physics-inspired models and brain dynamics. Validation involved numerical simulations and small-scale, proof-of-concept demonstrations in magnetoencephalography (MEG) source imaging13, showing that the method captures spatiotemporal features that cannot be detected with conventional analyses3–6.
Early empirical validations. We showed that kinetic energy derived from cortical flow segments whole-brain activity into stable states (low kinetic energy) and transitional states (high energy), with stable states aligning with key perceptual and motor events during a hand-eye coordination task4. At finer time scales, cortical flow captured stimulus-driven directionality in visual cortex and revealed the emergence of steady-state flows that converged toward attractor-like dynamics after stimulus onset4. Together, these results established that cortical flow can reveal both global coordination of whole-brain dynamics and local propagation features that are not detected with conventional analyses.
Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition (HHD). We combined cortical flow with a Riemannian HHD to decompose velocity fields into divergent sources, convergent sinks, and wavelike harmonic propagation paths6, yielding intuitive motifs of cortical dynamics. We demonstrated feasibility by identifying somatosensory flow sources within 20 ms of tactile stimulation, and by tracing epileptic spike propagation from onset to sink in an MEG case-study. Other groups have since adopted similar frameworks in developmental and clinical contexts1–3. These advances laid the groundwork for quantifying flowlines, recurrent source-sink motifs, and kinetic hierarchies, not to be scaled through the high-performance developments planned here.

Alignment with project aims.
	In Phase 1, we will extend these foundations to a scalable Global Cortical-Flow (GCF) framework that combines geodesic, surface-native flow estimation with joint cortex–time spectral representations to enable multiscale, frequency-specific propagation hierarchies and event-based descriptors (direction, speed, trajectories, and source–sink motifs).
	In Phase 2, we will use our curated Lifespan Cohort (n>1,700; ages 4–88) to chart normative variation in cortical-flow descriptors, incorporating demographic and behavioral covariates, and generating the first reference atlas of brain activity in motion.
	In Phase 3, we will apply cortical-flow methods to two epilepsy cohorts (pediatric n=121; adult n=300 with paired MEG+EEG), including iEEG available in n=117, to test whether flow descriptors enhance localization of epileptogenic regions, track propagating pathways, and predict surgical outcomes beyond current clinical standards.
Data resources. Our project is uniquely enabled by large-scale data already under our stewardship. The Open MEG Archive (OMEGA)41, created and maintained by our group, now hosts 1,800 MEG recordings from >640 participants, supports >1,000 registered users, and has powered over 120 publications. OMEGA will support all three Phases, from methods validation to atlas construction. We have already federated Human Connectome Project (HCP)39 and CamCAN40 data in prior work17,20,21, demonstrating our ability to harmonize across sites and integrate them into the Lifespan Cohort. Established partnerships further strengthen feasibility: Prof. Margot Taylor (SickKids/U of Toronto; Phase 2), contributes 401 pediatric and adult MEG/MRI datasets, Dr. Roy Dudley9,58–61 (Montreal Children’s Hospital, McGill) provides access to a rare pediatric epilepsy cohort and surgical outcomes; and Dr. Dang Nguyen (CHUM/U of Monrteal) contributes an independent adult epilepsy dataset with paired MEG and EEG and expanded iEEG benchmarking for cross-site and -modality generalization.
Software and dissemination. All methods will be developed and disseminated through Brainstorm, the open-source platform co-founded by Baillet and Leahy, supported for two decades through successive NIH R01 awards. As NIH support is now ending prematurely for Canadian researchers, this project is essential to secure continuity and consolidate Brainstorm’s core development in Canada. Brainstorm has >50,000 registered users and has supported >4,500 published studies. It integrates MEG/EEG source imaging with structural and diffusion MRI, intracranial EEG (iEEG), and other data types, along with advanced statistical tools, making it the ideal environment for disseminating cortical-flow modules and the normative atlas across all Phases. Dissemination will be amplified through Brainstorm’s established training resources (>45 international workshops, extensive online tutorials, and an active user forum with >60,000 posts).
We also co-founded neuromaps8, an open-source toolbox that provides curated reference maps of brain structure, function, and neurochemistry, and supports statistical comparison of spatial data across coordinate systems38. Neuromaps is now fully interoperable with Brainstorm and will be leveraged in Phases 2–3 to align cortical-flow descriptors with normative maps of cortical organization, connectivity, and molecular architecture.
In short, we are technically, scientifically, and logistically prepared to deliver on the three proposed Phases. This proposal is a direct continuation of proven innovations, now scaled to population and clinical relevance.
2. Budget Justification in Relation to Overall Funding
Prof. Baillet currently holds 12 active research grants as principal or co-investigator (> $50M in career funding from CIHR, NSERC, NIH, and others). None overlap with this proposal. Related grants either end before the start of this project or support distinct collaborations. The only partial overlap was an NIH R01 co-led with Prof. Leahy, originally expected to fund Brainstorm development until early 2026. However, recent NIH policy changes have terminated support for foreign components, including ours. The CIHR funds requested here are therefore critical to ensure continuity, in addition to supporting novel developments and studies.
The requested budget aligns with established funding levels and trainee support. It will sustain postdoctoral researchers, graduate students, data and computing services, our professional software developer, and training/dissemination activities. This investment ensures momentum, secures the timely repatriation of Brainstorm’s development to Canada, and guarantees that methodological breakthroughs transition into open, durable community resources and clinical applications.
